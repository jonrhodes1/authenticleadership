Your Archetype

What does this mean for you?

Your archetype describes your default mode, the style you fall back on under pressure. The real leverage comes when you understand how that archetype interacts with others in your team.

What if your team has all Archetypes covered?
+

A full spread of archetypes is a genuine strength, every critical leadership function has a natural owner. Navigators hold direction, Connectors hold relationships, Guardians hold stability. The risk is friction without shared language: people mistake difference for deficiency.

The opportunity: make the diversity visible. When people understand why a colleague leads differently, what feels like conflict often becomes complementarity.

What if your team has too many of the same Archetypes?
+

Concentration creates blind spots. A team of Navigators will excel at strategy and miss the human signals. A team of Energisers will generate momentum but struggle to consolidate it. The pattern tends to repeat, in meetings, in decisions, in the problems that never quite get solved.

The opportunity: name the gap. Knowing what's missing is the first step to compensating for it, either by developing range in existing members, or by being deliberate about who you bring in next.

What if your team is missing an Archetype?
+

A missing archetype doesn't mean the function disappears, it means it goes unowned. If no one is anchoring, the team drifts. If no one is synchronising, coordination becomes invisible overhead. Teams rarely name the gap; they just feel the drag.

The opportunity: gap awareness is leadership intelligence. Once you can name what's missing, you can make a conscious choice: who develops that capacity, who you bring in, or what process you put in place to cover it.

Part 2: The Three Systems

How Drive, Threat, and Soothing shape leadership under pressure

Three systems regulate how we respond to challenges:

  • Drive powers achievement, momentum, and doing.
  • Threat activates protection, vigilance, and alarm responses.
  • Soothing enables recovery, perspective, and connection.

These systems are not "good" or "bad"; they work together dynamically. Under pressure, they can become imbalanced. Understanding these patterns helps you lead with clarity, especially when facing project deadlines, stakeholder demands, ambiguity, or performance pressure.

02

The MI Spirit

A different philosophy for how we help people change.

Why our instinct to fix doesn't work

The Righting Reflex

When someone struggles, we try to fix it. This is the Righting Reflex, the instinct to correct, advise, and instruct. It comes from a good place. But research consistently shows that unsolicited advice activates resistance, not change.

When we tell people what to do, we take ownership of the solution. They lose ownership of the problem.

Advice / Fix
Telling, instructing,
correcting
→
Resistance
Defensiveness,
shutdown, compliance
Questions / Reflect
Exploring, drawing out,
listening deeply
→
Motivation
Insight, ownership,
genuine change

"People are generally better persuaded by the reasons they discovered themselves than by those which came from others."

Blaise Pascal
Miller & Rollnick, 2013

The Spirit of Motivational Interviewing

MI is a way of being with another person, rather than a set of techniques.

P
Partnership
You work with people, not on them. You are a collaborator, not an expert dispensing answers.
A
Acceptance
You recognise each person's intrinsic worth and their right to make their own choices, even when you disagree.
C
Compassion
You actively promote their wellbeing. Your agenda is their growth, not your comfort, not the quick fix.
E
Evocation
You draw out what people already know, value, and are capable of. Wisdom is in them, your job is to help them find it.

Miller, W.R., & Rollnick, S. (2013). Motivational Interviewing: Helping people change (3rd ed.). Guilford Press.

Traditional approach vs. MI

From expert to explorer

"Tell them what to do" → "Ask what they think"
"Give advice" → "Offer reflections"
"Explain the consequences" → "Explore what matters to them"
"Be the authority" → "Be the witness"
Being powerfully curious is an active choice. High standards and genuine curiosity work together.

03

OARS

Four skills. One conversation.

O Open Questions A Affirmations R Reflective Listening S Summaries
O

Open Questions

Open questions cannot be answered with yes or no. They invite the other person to think, explore, and speak at length. They move the conversation toward understanding rather than conclusion.

Closed Open
"Did you speak to them?" → "What happened when you spoke to them?"
"Are you okay?" → "What's been going on for you lately?"
"Do you understand?" → "What makes sense to you so far, and what doesn't?"
"Is there a problem?" → "What's been most challenging about this?"

Notice that open questions start with what, how, tell me about. "Why" questions can feel interrogative, use with care.

A

Affirmations

An affirmation differs from praise. Praise is performance-focused: "You did well." An affirmation is character-focused: it recognises who the person is, not just what they did. That matters because affirmations build the person's belief that they can do the hard thing.

Praise Affirmation
"You did well on that." → "You stayed calm under a lot of pressure there."
"Good effort." → "You clearly care deeply about getting this right."
That took real courage to say in that meeting.
I've noticed how consistently you show up for your team.
The fact you're asking these questions tells me a lot about your commitment.
R

Reflective Listening

A reflection is a statement. It mirrors back what you heard, showing understanding rather than interrogating it. When people feel genuinely heard, they open up further.

Simple reflection Complex reflection
Repeats or lightly rephrases what was said. Adds meaning, emotion, or direction, going slightly beyond what was literally said.
"So you're feeling overloaded." "It sounds like you want to do a good job, but the volume of work is making that feel impossible right now."

Useful stems

"It sounds like you…"
"You're wondering whether…"
"What I'm hearing is that you…"
"Part of you wants to… and part of you…"
S

Summaries

Summaries collect key themes and demonstrate that you've been listening. They create space for the other person to correct, add, or reflect, and they move the conversation forward with purpose.

Collecting

Draws together what's been said so far. Creates a sense of being heard.

Linking

Connects current content to something said earlier. Shows you've been tracking the whole conversation.

Transitional

Moves the conversation to a new phase. Signals that you've captured what matters and it's time to act.

Example: Transitional Summary

"So, you want to stay in the role, you feel the workload has been genuinely difficult to manage, and you have some ideas about what would help. Can we talk about those now?"

Scenario 1 of 3

The Missed Deadlines

Alex has been missing deadlines, submitting teaching materials late, and completing moderation notes incompletely. As their manager, you've requested a meeting. Alex says there's "a lot going on."

Assertive communication Observable evidence Support & accountability
Scenario 1

Version 1: Where it goes wrong

Read through the conversation. Notice the communication style, the impact on Alex, and what's missing.

Version 1
Manager:Alex, we need to talk. Things are not going well. Deadlines missed, late materials, errors, it's causing real problems.
Alex:I know I've fallen behind. There's been a lot going on.
Manager:We all have things going on, Alex. It's no excuse. At this level you should be able to manage your workload without constant reminders.
Alex:I am honestly trying my best…
Manager:Your best clearly isn't good enough right now. Students are complaining. Other colleagues are picking up the slack. It's not acceptable.
Pause for group discussion
Discuss

Before seeing the improved version, what would you change?

Version 2: The MI approach
Manager:Thanks for meeting with me, Alex. I want this to be a supportive conversation where we're clear about what's happening and how we get things back on track together.
Alex:Okay.
Manager:Over the last six weeks we've had two late feedback returns, materials uploaded on the morning of delivery, and incomplete moderation notes. I know you're committed to the work, so I wanted to understand, what's been getting in the way?
Alex:I've been covering the extra labs and I've had some personal stuff going on. I thought I could manage it all.
Manager:That sounds like a lot to carry. At the same time, we do need to meet the key standards, students and colleagues rely on them. So let's look at what support would actually help you get there. What would make the biggest difference right now?
Alex:If one of the labs could be covered for a few weeks, and I had a catch-up day, I could reset.
Manager:We can do that. Let's agree: temporary lab cover, protected time, weekly check-ins, and a review at two and four weeks. How does that feel?
Alex:Much better. Thank you.
OARS in Action

Open Question: "What's been getting in the way?" drew out Alex's real situation.
Reflection: "That sounds like a lot to carry" acknowledged Alex without excusing the impact.
Affirmation: "I know you're committed" preserved Alex's dignity and motivation.

Scenario 2 of 3

The Disruptive Voice

Taylor is a senior team member who regularly dismisses new ideas in meetings, "that will never work", "we tried that years ago." After recent meetings, other team members have stopped contributing. Taylor doesn't seem aware of the impact.

Naming behaviour without judgement Describing impact on others Holding team standards
Scenario 2

Version 1: Where it goes wrong

Notice how vagueness protects the manager but leaves the problem unresolved.

Version 1
Manager:I just wanted a quick word, some people feel meetings have been a bit negative lately.
Taylor:Right…
Manager:It's probably just stress. Maybe try to be a bit more positive.
Taylor:Okay…
Pause for group discussion
Discuss

What would a leader using OARS do differently here?

Version 2: The MI approach
Manager:Thanks for making time. I want to talk about the team meetings because I've noticed something that's having an impact, and I think it's worth us talking about directly.
Taylor:Okay.
Manager:In the last two meetings, when new ideas were raised, you said "that will never work" and "we tried that years ago." After those moments, people stopped contributing. What's your read on how those conversations went?
Taylor:I didn't realise it was coming across like that. I was just being honest.
Manager:I know, and your experience and honest challenge is something the team genuinely needs. The impact right now is that people are holding back. We're losing good thinking because of it. How do we keep your input and keep others engaged at the same time?
Taylor:I could ask more questions instead of shutting ideas down straight away.
Manager:That would make a real difference. Let's try that in the next meeting and check in afterwards. How does that sound?
OARS in Action

Open Question: "What's your read on how those conversations went?" invited Taylor's perspective before correction.
Affirmation: "Your experience and honest challenge is something the team genuinely needs", character, not performance.
Observable behaviour: Specific instances named, not personality attacked.

Scenario 3 of 3

The Defensive Response

Jordan has missed two key deadlines. When you raise it, Jordan immediately becomes defensive, escalating quickly. They feel they are constantly being criticised, and that last-minute work added by others is the real cause.

Self-regulation under pressure De-escalation Empathy with clarity
Scenario 3

Version 1: Where it goes wrong

Notice the escalation cycle and the moment the conversation becomes unrecoverable.

Version 1
Jordan:You're always on my case.
Manager:That's not true, I treat everyone the same. Look at last month's figures,
Jordan:You never listen to me. You've already decided I'm the problem.
Manager:That's completely unfair. I'm trying to do my job and you're being defensive.
Jordan:Because you don't understand the pressure I'm under.
Manager:Everyone is under pressure. You're not the only one. You just need to take responsibility.
Jordan:There's no point talking about this.
Manager:Fine, but the deadlines still have to be met.
Pause for group discussion
Discuss

This is the hardest scenario, someone in Threat, escalating fast.

Version 2: The MI approach
Jordan:You're always on my case.
Manager:(calm, deliberate pace)
Manager:That isn't my intention. I do need to talk about the last two deadlines, they affect the whole team. But let's slow this down. What's been most frustrating from your side?
Jordan:You never listen to me. You've already decided I'm the problem.
Manager:I hear that you feel unheard. I want to understand, what's been going on for you with these deadlines?
Jordan:I keep getting urgent work added at the last minute, and then I'm blamed when other things slip.
Manager:So the last-minute priorities are making it harder to meet the original deadlines. That's important, and it's something we can fix. At the same time, the deadlines still matter because other people's work depends on them. Can we look at both problems together?
Jordan:Yes. I just don't want to feel like I'm constantly being criticised.
Manager:That makes sense. My intention is to solve the problem, not criticise you. So let's agree on how urgent work gets prioritised, which deadlines are fixed, and when we check in if things start to slip.
OARS in Action

Reflection first: "I hear that you feel unheard", acknowledging Jordan's emotion before the task de-escalated the threat system.
Open Question: "What's been most frustrating?" created space rather than argument.
Slowing the pace is itself a de-escalation tool, the manager's calm tone set a new register for the conversation.
